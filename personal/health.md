# Health

### Convenience is the enemy of health

### The optimal morning routine

**Morning Light Exposure:**

Get natural light exposure within 1 hour of waking. This aligns your circadian rhythm, regulates cortisol levels, and enhances mood, focus, and alertness.

If sunlight isn't available, use bright artificial light or light therapy devices.

**Cortisol and Body Temperature:**

Cortisol spikes early in the day are healthy and help set your body's temperature and alertness rhythms.

Delayed light exposure shifts cortisol rhythms, contributing to sleep issues and mood disturbances.

**Physical Effects of Sunlight:**

Morning sunlight on the eyes and skin increases dopamine, testosterone, and estrogen levels, boosting mood, energy, and overall motivation.

Sunlight stimulates the skin as an endocrine organ, leading to enhanced hormone regulation.

Sunlight does more than just help produce vitamin D; infrared light penetrates deeply into the body, stimulating mitochondrial function and generating melatonin within the cells acting as an internal antioxidant

These effects contribute to improved cellular energy and overall health—even on cloudy days, time spent outdoors offers health benefits that go beyond synthesized vitamin D

**Caffeine Timing:**

Delay caffeine intake by 60–90 minutes after waking to allow adenosine (a sleep-pressure molecule) to clear naturally. This prevents afternoon energy crashes.

**Hydration and Exercise:**

Hydrate immediately upon waking and engage in physical activity to further clear adenosine and raise body temperature.

**Cold Exposure Benefits:**

Brief cold exposure (e.g., cold showers or ice baths) increases dopamine and adrenaline, enhancing mood, focus, and resilience.

Short cold exposure can help manage stress and improve mental health through neurochemical responses.

**Maximizing Body Temperature Cycles:**

Keep your environment cool at night for better sleep.

In the morning, increase body temperature through movement, light exposure, and other activities to align with your natural circadian rhythm.

**Circadian Alignment:**

Regularly anchor your internal clock through consistent wake times and morning habits. This improves energy levels, metabolism, and sleep quality.

**Seasonal Adjustments:**

In darker months or regions, double the time spent outdoors in daylight, or use light therapy to maintain mood and hormonal balance.

**Practical Implementation:**

Aim for 10–30 minutes of light exposure daily, prioritize hydration, delay caffeine, and incorporate cold exposure and exercise to optimize your morning routine.

Deep Sleep (Stage N3)

Duration: \~13-23% of total sleep (\~1-2 hours for 8 hours of sleep).

Function: Physical restoration, growth hormone release, immune strengthening, and cellular repair.

Optimal Characteristics: More deep sleep occurs earlier in the night.

Decreases with age (younger individuals have more deep sleep).

If you miss the optimal window for deep sleep, you may lose it entirely for the night.

Missing deep sleep leads to a cascading effect of poor health choices, increased stress, and worsened cognitive function.

REM Sleep (Rapid Eye Movement)

Duration: \~20-25% of total sleep (\~1.5-2 hours for 8 hours of sleep).

Function: Memory consolidation, learning, emotional processing, and dreaming.

Optimal Characteristics: REM sleep is concentrated in the latter half of the night.

Increases in duration with each successive sleep cycle.

Dreams are experienced during the REM (rapid eye movement) sleep stage, where the brain is highly active.

Dreams may have multiple functions that influence success, intelligence, and survival and can serve as memory replay, problem-solving, social simulation, and creative inspiration.

“It’s during REM sleep that your brain replays emotional experiences from the day—but without the same neurochemical stress reaction you had during the actual event.”

Function:

REM sleep reprocesses emotionally charged memories and removes or blunts the emotional sting while preserving the memory itself.

Mechanism:

During REM, norepinephrine (a stress-related chemical) is switched off in the brain.

This allows the brain to “revisit” emotional experiences in a low-stress environment.

The result: The memory remains, but the emotional intensity is reduced, making the experience less triggering the next day or in the future.

Example Walker gives:

If something deeply upsetting happens, and you get a good night’s sleep, it often feels more manageable the next day—not because you forgot it, but because REM sleep has helped process it.

Consequence of sleep deprivation:

When you don't get sufficient REM sleep, emotional reactivity increases.

You’re more likely to feel overwhelmed or irrationally affected by experiences.

This is linked to mood disorders, including depression, anxiety, and PTSD.

Core (Light) Sleep (Stages N1 and N2)

Duration: \~50-60% of total sleep (\~4-5 hours for 8 hours of sleep).

Function: Prepares the body for deep and REM sleep, regulates cardiovascular and metabolic processes.

Optimal Characteristics: Light sleep is foundational and occupies the largest proportion of the sleep cycle.

Acceptable Awakenings:

Brief awakenings (lasting a few seconds to a couple of minutes) are normal and typically occur at the end of a sleep cycle.

More than 4-5 prolonged awakenings (over 5 minutes) or a wake time exceeding 30 minutes in total may indicate disrupted sleep.

1. The Magnesium and Sleep Myth

* Brain Barrier Issue: Most forms of magnesium do not cross the blood-brain barrier. Since sleep is a brain process, many supplements are essentially creating "expensive urine" \[00:00].
* Effective Form: While most forms are ineffective for direct sleep generation, there is one specific form of magnesium (likely Magnesium L-Threonate, though not explicitly named in this snippet) that has evidence for crossing into the brain \[00:20].

2. Melatonin: Use and Misconceptions

* The "Starting Official" Metaphor: Melatonin is a hormone of darkness; it tells your body it’s nighttime, but it does not actually generate or participate in the sleep process itself \[22:04].
* Limited Efficacy: Meta-analyses show melatonin only improves the speed of falling asleep by about 3.4 minutes and sleep efficiency by 2.2%, making it only slightly more effective than a placebo for many \[22:24].
* Danger of Supra-Physiological Doses: Many over-the-counter doses (10-20mg) are far higher than what the body naturally releases. This can cause a "melatonin fog" in the morning, making you feel groggy because your body still thinks it is pitch black outside \[23:43].

3. New Sleep Science and Innovations

* New Class of Medication: Walker mentions the invention of a new class of sleeping medication (Dual Orexin Receptor Antagonists or DORAs) that he actually favors, as they work differently than traditional sedatives by "turning off" wakefulness rather than "knocking out" the brain \[00:36].

Strategies for Improving Sleep Quality

Low-carbohydrate or ketogenic diets can increase baseline cortisol levels because the body must mobilize glucose from stored sources. Including starchy carbohydrates (like rice, oatmeal, or sourdough) in the final meal of the day can suppress cortisol and improve the ability to fall and stay asleep.

While very low-carb diets may reduce brain fog for some, the resulting "wired but tired" feeling and sleep fragmentation can negate those benefits. Finding a balance of carbohydrates that supports both mental clarity and sleep is often necessary.

Engaging the vestibular system through controlled eye movements can help the brain transition into sleep. A recommended sequence involves:

Closing the eyes and moving them slowly from side to side.

Moving eyes in counterclockwise and clockwise circles.

Moving eyes up and down.

Performing a "faux cross-eyed" look toward the bridge of the nose.

Exhaling deeply to slow the heart rate.

### Sitting and ergonomics

**Ergonomics** There is no "best" posture while sitting or standing. We should change positions and move frequently in order to load tissues in body evenly

Optimal Sitting Posture: The optimal sitting posture aims for the least stressful position for joints that is easy to hold for an extended period. It is described as the neutral position, characterized by the natural curves in the spine and shoulders in line with the ears.

Legs and Lower Back: To achieve a neutral position, it's important to bring your feet below your hips, moving the center of mass back to the vertical axis. This reduces the need to activate lower back muscles and prevents sliding forward.

Arms and Thorax: Maintaining a 90-degree angle at the elbows is essential to keep the shoulders and thoracic spine in a neutral position. A setup with the keyboard close to your body helps enforce this posture.

Head and Neck: To keep the head and neck in a neutral position, the top of the screen should be in line with your eyes. This prevents bending the neck for extended periods

2. The Problem with Modern Footwear

* Tapered Toe Boxes: Most modern shoes, especially for children and women, are too narrow at the toes. This changes the foot's structure, forcing toes to curl or crumple \[08:49].
* Heel-to-Toe Drop: Shoes with a higher heel (even "mini" heels in trainers) shorten the calf muscles and put excessive pressure on the front of the foot, which can lead to back, hip, and leg issues \[10:09].
* Excessive Cushioning: While soft soles feel good initially, they act as a sensory barrier. The foot is a sensory organ with thousands of receptors; too much cushion results in a loss of "sensory acuity" and balance \[12:56].

3. Rethinking Foot Pain and Treatment

* Weakness vs. Injury: Much of the common heel pain (like plantar fasciitis) stems from foot weakness. Specifically, the weakness of the flexor digitorum brevis muscle often correlates with heel pain \[18:33].
* The Problem with Orthotics (Insoles): While inserts can help offload pain in the acute phase, they should not be a permanent solution. Over-reliance on them leads to muscle atrophy ("if you don't use it, you lose it") \[17:42].
* The "And" Conversation: Treatment should focus on using orthotics temporarily and strengthening the foot simultaneously to create an exit strategy for the insert \[17:48].

4. Foot Function and Natural Structure

* Natural Foundations: Observations of barefoot populations show feet that are wider, thicker, and have a greater range of motion in the toes \[15:22].
* Load Bearing: The human foot is designed to handle four to six times your body weight when walking, but it requires appropriate loading and movement to maintain its strength \[03:51].

Tap Water vs. Well Water: Tap water from municipal systems in the Czech Republic is generally of good quality, as water companies remove many contaminants, but it still contains micropollutants like pesticides, microplastics, and chlorination by-products that standard treatments can’t fully eliminate. Well water is often of poorer quality, frequently contaminated with bacteria, high nitrates, ammonia, or seepage from cesspools and fertilized fields.

Organic pollutants like pesticides accumulate in fatty tissues, but it’s challenging to quantify their specific origin (water vs. food). Microplastics are difficult to detect but pervasive, contributing to health concerns.

Long-term exposure to low-level contaminants, especially endocrine disruptors, may affect hormonal systems, though direct causation is hard to prove due to multiple exposure sources.

While environmental awareness is reducing pesticide use, their long persistence (e.g., 20+ years in soil) means water quality improvements will be slow.

Polman remains cautiously optimistic, noting that water filtration will likely remain relevant due to ongoing challenges with intensive agriculture and historical industrial pollution.

Filtration Technologies

Activated Carbon Filters (e.g., pitcher filters): Effective for heavy metals and some organic compounds but require 20 minutes to 2 hours for proper absorption. Prolonged standing can lead to bacterial growth, so water should be used promptly.

Ultrafiltration: Removes bacteria, viruses, and microplastics but allows dissolved substances like nitrates and antibiotics to pass through, offering only partial purification.

Reverse Osmosis: Produces nearly pure water by removing almost all contaminants, including beneficial minerals, necessitating remineralization (e.g., mixing with mineral water) to avoid health risks from demineralized water. It’s ideal for highly contaminated sources (e.g., in India or for well water) or specific uses like dental offices.

Nanofiltration: A preferred method for household use, as it removes large organic molecules (pesticides, endocrine disruptors) and pathogens while retaining essential minerals like calcium and magnesium. It’s less drastic than reverse osmosis and suitable for tap water.

Recommended CO2 Levels Indoors

Ideal indoor CO2 concentration: 400–600 ppm (parts per million), which is close to outdoor ambient air levels.

Acceptable range for well-ventilated spaces: 600–1,000 ppm. Levels in this range typically indicate good air quality and ventilation.

Levels to avoid: CO2 levels above 1,000 ppm can indicate inadequate ventilation, potentially causing discomfort and reducing cognitive performance. At levels above 2,000 ppm, symptoms like drowsiness, headaches, and impaired thinking become more pronounced.

Impact of High CO2 Levels on Cognitive Functions

Reduced attention and decision-making ability: Studies suggest that even moderate elevations in CO2 (around 1,200–1,500 ppm) can impair cognitive functions, including focus, decision-making, and complex problem-solving.

Severe effects at very high levels: Prolonged exposure to levels above 2,500 ppm has been associated with significant cognitive decline and fatigue.

Importance of Ventilation to Control CO2 Levels

Ventilation is the most effective way to reduce indoor CO2 concentrations. Here's why:

Dilution of indoor air: Proper ventilation introduces fresh outdoor air, diluting indoor CO2 levels and maintaining air quality.

Removal of contaminants: Besides CO2, ventilation removes other pollutants, such as volatile organic compounds (VOCs) and airborne particles, improving overall air quality.

Improved health and productivity: Maintaining low CO2 levels enhances cognitive function, reduces the risk of fatigue, and promotes overall well-being.

Strategies to Improve Ventilation

Natural Ventilation:

Open windows and doors regularly to allow outdoor air to circulate.

Use cross-ventilation by opening windows on opposite sides of a room.

Mechanical Ventilation:

Install HVAC systems with proper filtration and air exchange rates.

Use exhaust fans in spaces like kitchens and bathrooms to reduce localized CO2 buildup.

Air Quality Monitoring:

Use CO2 monitors to measure and maintain indoor air quality within recommended levels.

Set alarms on devices to alert when levels exceed thresholds.

Long-term stay in temperatures around 28-30 °C, especially in the summer months without refrigeration, can have negative effects on health, performance and regeneration. From this point of view, the purchase of air conditioning is not only a comfortable but also a healthy investment.

1. Insufficient cooling of the body → chronic heat stress

The body tries to maintain an internal temperature of \~37 °C.

Long-term exposure to heat activates thermoregulatory stress:

increased blood circulation in the skin,

higher heart rate even at rest,

constant sweating (even light),

increased sympathetic nervous system.

The consequence: the body is constantly in a state of mild stress, not regeneration.

2. Poorer sleep (especially deep and REM)

High temperature interferes with the body's ability to go into deep sleep:

Deep (N3) sleep requires a drop in internal body temperature of \~1°C.

REM sleep is sensitive to thermal conditions – it is shorter and intermittent when it is warm.

Without quality sleep, the following decreases:

Growth hormone (GH) synthesis,

Repair of muscles and tissues,

Immune function,

Detoxification of the brain (glymphatic system).

3. Impaired hydration and electrolyte imbalance

At high temperatures, you lose water and sodium even without sweating on the outside.

Even mild dehydration (\~1-2%) affects:

performance

concentration

muscle regeneration (impaired transport of nutrients),

kidney function and metabolism.

4. Hormonal changes

Heat stress affects cortisol (stress hormone):

High cortisol = impaired recovery, poorer sleep, poorer muscle growth.

Heat is disrupted by melatonin – it is sensitive to temperature conditions.

Low melatonin = worse falling asleep, less deep sleep.

5. Reduced parasympathetic (regenerative) tone

Regeneration is controlled by the parasympathetic nervous system (rest, recovery).

In the heat, the sympathetic system is more active (stress, survival).

The consequence: the body "rides" all day in a mild fighting mode instead of recovery.

Consequences in the long run:

Lower levels of regenerative hormones (GH, testosterone, melatonin),

Higher resting heart rate,

Impaired sleep → impaired muscle recovery, mental acuity, immunity,

Chronic fatigue, irritability, lower motivation.

Solution = air conditioning

The impact of sunglasses on sun perception and skin adaptation

Wearing sunglasses is key to protecting your eyes from harmful UV rays. However, wearing dark sunglasses too often and for too long can have a negative impact on your body’s natural perception of sunlight. When you wear sunglasses, your brain receives a signal that it is dark, and your pupils remain dilated. This can lead to more harmful UV rays entering your eyes if your sunglasses do not have sufficient UV filters. Furthermore, the lack of natural daylight reaching your retina can affect melatonin production, which can affect your sleep and mood. Although the primary focus is on eye protection, some experts suggest that without direct exposure to the full spectrum of sunlight, the body may not adapt optimally to sunlight, which could also affect the skin's natural protective mechanisms, such as gradual tanning and vitamin D production. It is therefore important to find a balance between protecting the eyes and allowing the body to naturally perceive sunlight, for example by briefly spending time in the sun without glasses at safe times.

#### **Heart rate variability (HRV)**

is simply a measure of the variation in time between each heartbeat. This variation is controlled by a primitive part of the nervous system called the autonomic nervous system (ANS). It works behind the scenes, automatically regulating our heart rate, blood pressure, breathing, and digestion among other key tasks. The ANS is subdivided into two large components: the sympathetic and the parasympathetic nervous system, also known as the fight-or-flight mechanism and the relaxation response.

The brain is constantly processing information in a region called the hypothalamus. The ANS provides signals to the hypothalamus, which then instructs the rest of the body either to stimulate or to relax different functions. It responds not only to a poor night of sleep, or that sour interaction with your boss, but also to the exciting news that you got engaged, or to that delicious healthy meal you had for lunch. Our body handles all kinds of stimuli and life goes on. However, if we have persistent instigators such as stress, poor sleep, unhealthy diet, dysfunctional relationships, isolation or solitude, and lack of exercise, this balance may be disrupted, and your fight-or-flight response can shift into overdrive.

HRV may offer a noninvasive way to signal imbalances in the autonomic nervous system. Based on data gathered from many people, if the system is in more of a fight-or-flight mode, the variation between subsequent heartbeats tends to be lower. If the system is in more relaxed state, the variation between beats may be higher.

This suggests some interesting possibilities. People who have a high HRV may have greater cardiovascular fitness and may be more resilient to stress. HRV may also provide personal feedback about your lifestyle and help motivate those who are considering taking steps toward a healthier life. You might see a connection to HRV changes as you incorporate more mindfulness, meditation, sleep, and especially physical activity into your life. For those who love data and numbers, this could be a way to track how your nervous system is reacting not only to the environment, but also to your emotions, thoughts, and feelings.

## Engineering Reverse Aging

* Aging is primarily **loss of epigenetic information**, not mutation of DNA itself
* DNA stays mostly intact; what degrades is **gene expression control (epigenome)**
* Cells “forget” their role → leads to dysfunction and disease
* Aging is largely **modifiable**; lifestyle influences dominate over genetics
* Environmental inputs continuously rewrite epigenetic state
* **Hormesis is central mechanism**: controlled stress triggers repair pathways
* No stress → degeneration; excessive stress → damage; optimal stress → adaptation
* Major hormetic stressors:
  * fasting
  * physical exercise
  * cold exposure
  * heat exposure
* These stressors activate survival pathways including:
  * sirtuins
  * AMPK
  * mitochondrial biogenesis
* **Sirtuins require NAD⁺** to function → NAD⁺ availability is a bottleneck for repair
* NAD⁺ declines with age → repair systems weaken → damage accumulates
* Mitochondrial dysfunction is a **core driver of aging**
* Reduced mitochondrial efficiency → less energy + more oxidative stress
* Improving mitochondrial function is one of the highest leverage interventions
* Food acts as a **signaling system**, not just caloric input
* Plant stress compounds (xenohormesis) activate human defense pathways
* Example: compounds like sulforaphane trigger detox and protective gene expression
* Chronic inflammation and metabolic dysfunction accelerate epigenetic aging
* Many diseases (cancer, neurodegeneration, cardiovascular disease) are **downstream effects of aging processes**, not isolated causes
* Experimental work shows **epigenetic reprogramming can reverse aging markers**
* In animals, partial reprogramming restores tissue function and extends lifespan
* Aging may be biologically **reversible at the cellular level**, but human application is still early-stage
* Longevity strategy = maintain:
  * epigenetic stability
  * mitochondrial function
  * NAD⁺ levels
  * adaptive stress response
* Healthspan (functionality) is more important than raw lifespan

  * inflammation control
  * mitochondrial maintenance

With age:

* NAD⁺ levels **decline significantly**
* Result → reduced repair capacity + accumulation of damage

Key implication:
