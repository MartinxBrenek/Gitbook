1. Adaptation vs Overload

The body adapts to stress, but connective tissue (tendons, bones) adapts much slower than muscles or cardiovascular system.

Pain in feet for a week after running on concrete = overload, not healthy adaptation.

2. Concrete Surface

Hardest surface; impact forces reach 2.5–3× body weight.

Overloads plantar fascia, Achilles tendon, shins, knees, hips.

Even elite runners limit concrete running.

3. Shoes for Concrete

Use high-cushion shoes with 8–10 mm drop.

Avoid minimalist shoes unless highly conditioned.

Replace shoes every 500–700 km.

Examples: Hoka Clifton, Asics Gel Nimbus, Nike ZoomX Invincible, Brooks Glycerin.

4. Better Surfaces

Grass or dirt paths: best for tendons and bones.

Track (tartan): good for speed sessions.

Concrete: occasional use only.

5. Summary

Foot pain = overload, not progress.

For cardiovascular gains, softer surfaces are just as effective.

### Recovery / nervous system and training

Muscle control is governed by the nervous system through:

Upper motor neurons (deliberate movement, brain to spinal cord)

Lower motor neurons (spinal cord to muscles, release acetylcholine)

Central Pattern Generators (CPGs) (rhythmic, unconscious movement like walking)

Muscles recruit motor units from low to high threshold based on load and fatigue.

More motor units are recruited with continued effort, not just heavy weight.

Motor unit recruitment leads to muscle adaptation

Effective Training Volume

5 sets/week per muscle group = maintenance

10–15+ sets/week = growth and strength gains

Volume should be distributed across sessions, not necessarily done all at once.

Only \~10% of sets should be to failure; most should stop short to allow more total volume.

Hypertrophy: Focus on isolating specific muscles and generating intense local contractions.

Strength: Use compound movements and focus on increasing load over time.

Hypertrophy (muscle size) and strength are related but distinct.

Strength can improve without size increase, but hypertrophy usually boosts strength.

To build muscle or strength, heavy weights are not required.

30–80% of your 1RM (1-rep max) is sufficient.

Nervous system and Fatigue

The nervous system and muscles play different roles in training. While muscles handle the physical work (contracting, lifting, holding), the nervous system is the communication pathway that controls, coordinates, and powers these movements.

It’s easy to exhaust the nervous system with intense, heavy, or high-volume training, especially with compound movements that require a high level of coordination, balance, and intensity.

Muscles, however, can recover relatively quickly if targeted correctly, and even after a hard workout, they can often handle some more isolated or moderate exercises without overly taxing the nervous system.

When the nervous system is over-fatigued, performance declines, recovery is slower, and the body struggles to keep up with training demands.

Exhausting the nervous system leads to symptoms like poor movement quality, mental fatigue, and lack of focus, which can increase the risk of injury.

For clients, nervous system fatigue often leads to burnout, as it makes them feel more mentally and physically drained, without always contributing to muscle growth.

Many personal trainers accidentally push clients into nervous system fatigue by prescribing too many compound exercises (like deadlifts, squats, etc.) or by using unnecessarily high weights and volume.

#### Recovery indicators

**Grip strength:** Morning grip test indicates nervous system recovery. Decreases in grip strength can indicate poor neuromuscular recovery, overtraining, or central nervous system fatigue.

Several large-scale studies show that lower grip strength is associated with higher mortality, cardiovascular disease, and disability in aging populations.

**CO₂ discard test:**

After a deep breath hold and full exhale, time how long you can continue exhaling.

<25–30 seconds = poor recovery; >40 seconds = good recovery, >60 to 120s = nervous system is fully recovered

If your body signals fatigue—through persistent soreness, sluggish performance, or elevated resting heart rate—then recovery shouldn’t be optional. Giving yourself permission to rest is often more productive than pushing through.

Use active recovery mindfully

Gentle, low-impact movement (like walking or stretching) can enhance blood circulation and recovery. However, it shouldn’t morph into a full training session—especially under the guise of “just staying active.”

Balance discipline with recovery

Fitness progress hinges on a foundation of disciplined training—but overtraining can derail performance. Integrating rest days into your plan sustains long-term adaptation.

Listen to your body and context

Poor sleep, high work stress, or other life pressures aren’t excuses—they’re cues to adjust training intensity or scale back. A well-rested athlete performs better across the board.

Cold shower after workout?

It depends on your goals, but generally, cold showers immediately after training are not recommended if your primary goal is muscle growth or strength gains.

Inflammation’s Role: After intense exercise, your muscles experience microtears, triggering an inflammatory response. This inflammation is a natural part of the repair process, as it signals the body to send nutrients and immune cells to the damaged tissue, promoting recovery and adaptation (e.g., muscle growth). Cytokines and other inflammatory markers help facilitate this process.

Cold Showers and Inflammation: Cold exposure, like a cold shower, causes vasoconstriction (narrowing of blood vessels), which reduces blood flow to the muscles. This can decrease the acute inflammatory response by limiting the influx of immune cells and inflammatory mediators to the area. Studies, such as those on cold-water immersion (CWI), show that cold exposure can reduce swelling and muscle soreness (DOMS) by dampening inflammation (e.g., a 2017 study in The Journal of Physiology found CWI reduced inflammatory markers post-exercise).

Effects of Reducing Inflammation Post-Training

Pros:

Reduced Soreness: Cold showers can alleviate delayed onset muscle soreness (DOMS) by reducing inflammation and swelling, making you feel better faster. A 2016 meta-analysis in Sports Medicine confirmed that cold-water immersion helps reduce DOMS after exercise.

Faster Perceived Recovery: Athletes often report feeling more refreshed after cold exposure, which can be beneficial for performance in subsequent sessions, especially during high-frequency training (e.g., tournaments).

Cons:

Blunted Adaptation: Inflammation drives muscle hypertrophy (growth) and strength gains by signaling pathways like mTOR, which are crucial for protein synthesis. Suppressing inflammation with cold exposure may hinder these adaptations. A 2019 study in The Journal of Physiology found that regular post-exercise cold-water immersion reduced long-term muscle hypertrophy and strength gains in resistance-trained individuals compared to a control group.

Reduced Blood Flow: While vasoconstriction reduces swelling, it also slows the delivery of nutrients and oxygen to muscles, potentially delaying repair. A 2021 study in Frontiers in Physiology noted that cold exposure might impair the anabolic response by limiting nutrient delivery during the critical post-exercise window.

Strength or Hypertrophy Goals: If your primary goal is muscle growth or strength, you want to allow inflammation to occur naturally. A 2023 review in Exercise and Sport Sciences Reviews highlighted that chronic use of cold exposure post-training can blunt the signaling pathways (e.g., IGF-1, mTOR) needed for muscle adaptation.

Long-Term Recovery: Consistently reducing inflammation might delay the full repair process, as the body needs time to rebuild stronger tissue. A 2020 study in European Journal of Applied Physiology suggested that natural inflammation should be allowed for optimal long-term recovery.
