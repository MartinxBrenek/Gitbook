# Nutrition

### Macronutrients — Overview

Avoid overconsumption of macronutrients:

* Proteins: \~1–1.5 g/kg is an optimal general range. Athletes: up to 2 g/kg typical; peak athletes can go up to \~3 g/kg body weight.
* Fats: pay attention to omega-3 / omega-6 balance.
* Carbs: if you expect low activity on a given day, lower carb intake.

{% hint style="info" %}
Cravings for sweets and processed foods often indicate micronutrient deficits — when nutrient needs are met, preference for sweets usually decreases.
{% endhint %}

***

### Carbohydrates

**Definition**

* Organic compounds (mono- to polysaccharides) formed in plants via photosynthesis (carbon + water).
* Plants use glucose to build structures (cellulose/fiber), store energy (starch), and attract animals (fructose in fruit).
* Humans digest plant foods to obtain glucose for cells.

**Primary groups**

* Monosaccharides: glucose, fructose, galactose.
* Disaccharides: sucrose, lactose, maltose.
* Oligosaccharides (3–9 units): maltodextrins, raffinose.
* Polysaccharides (>9 units): starch (amylose & amylopectin), glycogen, cellulose.

**Energy & function**

* 4 kcal/g.
* Primary fuel for brain, muscles, RBCs.
* Storage: glycogen (liver for blood glucose regulation; muscle for exercise).
* Excess converted into fat (lipogenesis).

**Types**

* Simple carbs (sugars): mono- & disaccharides. Fast digestion → rapid blood sugar spikes (high GI). Found in fruit, milk, honey, candy, refined sugar.
* Complex carbs (starch & fiber): long chains. Slow digestion → sustained energy. Found in whole grains, legumes, vegetables.
* Fiber (indigestible carbohydrate):
  * Soluble: forms gel, lowers cholesterol & blood sugar (oats, beans, fruit).
  * Insoluble: adds bulk, aids bowel movements (whole grains, seeds, skins).
  * Benefits: digestion, blood sugar stabilization, satiety, lower chronic disease risk.

**Other functions**

* Brain fuel: glucose main energy source for the brain.
* Exercise: glycogen crucial for high-intensity work.
* Protein-sparing: adequate carbs prevent protein breakdown.
* Metabolic health: excess refined carbs linked to elevated HbA1c, prediabetes, metabolic syndrome, inflammation.

**Dietary considerations**

* Recommended intake: 45–65% of total daily calories (context-dependent).
* Glycemic Index (GI): 0–100 scale. Low GI → slower, steadier energy (e.g., oats, legumes). High GI → quick spikes (e.g., white bread, candy).
* Combine carbs with protein/fat/fiber to slow absorption.
* Move after eating to help muscle glucose uptake.
* For athletes: carbs are vital for high-intensity performance; complex carbs preferred, but processed carbs (gels) can be useful during races.

**Myths & notes**

* "All carbs are bad" is false — impact depends on amount, quality, and lifestyle.
* All sugars (fruit, honey, refined) are processed similarly metabolically; whole fruit is preferable because fiber slows absorption.
* Commercial honey can be mostly sugar; its metabolic effect is similar to other sugars.
* Low-percentage chocolate cravings may indicate excessive sugar reliance or metabolic dysregulation.

**Practical glucose hacks**

* Savory breakfast if not exercising in morning.
* Combine carbs with protein/fat/fiber.
* Move after meals.
* If craving sweets, prefer dessert after a meal rather than between meals.
* 1 tbsp vinegar before meals can reduce glucose spikes (\~30–45%).

### Proteins

**Definition & structure**

* Macromolecules of amino acids linked by peptide bonds. Short chains (<20–30 residues) often called peptides.
* Sequence determined by genetic code; structure dictates function. Post-translational modifications alter properties.

**Energy & function**

* 4 kcal/g.
* Build and repair tissues (muscle, organs, skin, hair, nails).
* Essential for enzymes, hormones, transport, immune function, fluid & acid-base balance, and structural components (collagen, keratin).

**Amino acids**

* 9 Essential Amino Acids (EAAs): histidine, isoleucine, leucine, lysine, methionine, phenylalanine, threonine, tryptophan, valine.
* Non-essential and conditionally essential amino acids exist.
* Leucine is critical for activating mTOR and muscle protein synthesis.

**Dietary considerations**

* Complete proteins: contain all EAAs — mainly animal sources and a few plant sources (quinoa, soy, buckwheat).
* Incomplete proteins: lack one or more EAAs — most plant proteins.
* Complementary proteins: combine plant sources across the day (e.g., rice + beans).

**Recommended intake**

* Sedentary: \~0.8 g/kg.
* Active/athletes: \~1.2–2.2 g/kg (individual needs vary). The content referenced athletes using 2–3 g/kg/day as an upper range in some contexts.

**Protein digestion**

* Begins in stomach (pepsin) and continues in small intestine (pancreatic enzymes) → amino acids absorbed into bloodstream.

{% hint style="info" %}
Peptides are short amino-acid chains and act as signaling molecules; terms protein vs peptide depend on chain length.
{% endhint %}

### Fats (Lipids)

**Definition**

* In nutrition, fats commonly refer to triglycerides (esters of fatty acids). The broader term lipids includes phospholipids, sterols (cholesterol), waxes, and free fatty acids.

**Energy & function**

* 9 kcal/g — most energy-dense macronutrient.
* Concentrated energy, insulation, organ protection, absorption of fat‑soluble vitamins (A, D, E, K).
* Stored as triglycerides in adipose tissue.
* Essential fatty acids (omega-3, omega-6) must be obtained from diet.

**Key lipid facts**

* All fats are lipids, but not all lipids are fats.
* Cholesterol is a lipid (not a fat) and is essential; body produces \~80% of cholesterol.

**Cholesterol & lipoproteins**

* LDL ("bad"): can deposit in vessel walls → plaque, increased heart disease risk.
* HDL ("good"): carries cholesterol to liver for excretion.
* Dietary cholesterol has minimal impact on overall cholesterol levels; the body adjusts endogenous production.
* Elevated cholesterol can also result from excessive sugar intake (liver converts excess sugar into fat and cholesterol).

**Fat types**

* Saturated fat: solid at room temp. Found in animal products and some plant oils (coconut, palm). Can raise LDL. Limit to ≤10% of calories (\~22 g on a 2000 kcal diet).
* Trans fat: industrial hydrogenation — raises LDL, lowers HDL; avoid.
* Unsaturated fats:
  * Monounsaturated fats (MUFAs): olive oil, avocado, nuts — beneficial.
  * Polyunsaturated fats (PUFAs): includes omega-3, omega-6, omega-9.

**Omega fatty acids**

* Omega-3: anti-inflammatory; EPA & DHA (fish) important; ALA (plant) converts poorly to EPA/DHA. Aim \~250–500 mg EPA/DHA; 1.1–1.6 g ALA commonly recommended.
* Omega-6: necessary, but excess relative to omega-3 promotes inflammation.
* Omega-9: non-essential, beneficial (olive oil, avocado).

**Omega-3 vs omega-6 balance**

* Ideal omega-3:omega-6 ratio \~1:1 to 1:4; Western diets can reach 1:20 or worse.
* Refined vegetable oils (sunflower, corn, soybean) are high in omega-6.
* Extra virgin olive oil and ghee are low in omega-6 and preferable for cooking.

**Cooking fats by heat level**

* High heat: avocado oil or ghee (heat-stable).
* Medium heat: virgin olive oil, avocado oil, ghee.
* Low/no heat: extra virgin olive oil for flavor & polyphenols.

**General recommendation**

* Fats \~20–35% of daily calories.
* Prioritize unsaturated fats; limit saturated and avoid trans fats.
* Pair fats with proteins and complex carbs for nutrient diversity.

### Micronutrients — Vitamins & Minerals

**Micronutrients** are essential vitamins and minerals required in small amounts for metabolic functions, immune function, and development.

### Vitamins — Overview

* Fat-soluble: A, D, E, K — stored in fatty tissues; can accumulate → toxicity risk from excess (hypervitaminosis).
* Water-soluble: B-complex and C — not stored extensively; excess excreted in urine.

Vitamin highlights

* Vitamin A (retinol): animal sources. Toxicity possible at high intakes of preformed retinol. Beta-carotene (plant provitamin A) converts to retinol as needed and has negligible toxicity from food sources.

Table — Vitamin A sources and toxicity risk

| Source                           |                          Form | Toxicity risk  | Counted toward UL? |
| -------------------------------- | ----------------------------: | -------------- | ------------------ |
| Ghee, eggs, liver, dairy         | Preformed vitamin A (retinol) | High at excess | ✅ Yes              |
| Paprika, sweet potatoes, carrots |  Provitamin A (beta-carotene) | Very low       | ❌ No (from food)   |

Definitions

* Retinol = preformed vitamin A.
* Beta-carotene = provitamin A (converted to retinol).
* RE / RAE: older/newer units for vitamin A activity.

Other vitamins (summary)

* B1 (thiamine): energy metabolism, nerve function.
* B2 (riboflavin): energy production, growth.
* B3 (niacin): metabolism, skin health.
* B5 (pantothenic acid): energy production, compound synthesis.
* B6 (pyridoxine): protein metabolism, neurotransmitter synthesis.
* B7 (biotin): metabolism, hair/skin/nails.
* B9 (folate): DNA synthesis, cell division.
* B12 (cobalamin): nerve function, red blood cell production (animal sources).
* Vitamin C: antioxidant, immune support, collagen synthesis. Heat-sensitive; cooking reduces content.
* Vitamin D (D2 plant / D3 animal & skin synthesis): bone health, immune function. Typical D3 recommended intake 1000–5000 IU daily depending on blood levels. Pair with Vitamin K2 (MK-7) to direct calcium to bones (K2: 100–200 mcg daily recommended when supplementing D3).
* Vitamin E: antioxidant.
* Vitamin K1 (phylloquinone): plant-based, blood clotting.
* Vitamin K2 (menaquinone, MK-7 emphasized): animal & fermented foods; activates proteins to direct calcium to bones and away from arteries. MK-7 has better bioavailability and longer half-life. Observational data links higher K2 to lower heart disease and arterial calcification.

### Minerals & Electrolytes

**Electrolytes** are charged minerals essential for nutrient transport, hydration, nerve/muscle function, and more.

Major minerals

* Sodium (Na+): fluid balance, nerve/muscle function (salt, processed foods).
* Potassium (K+): fluid balance, nerve/muscle function (bananas, potatoes, legumes).
* Calcium (Ca2+): bone health, muscle/nerve function, clotting (dairy, some vegetables, fortified foods). Recommended range \~800–1200 mg/day; some suggest 600–800 mg may be sufficient with proper cofactors and activity. Excessive intake can be harmful.
* Magnesium (Mg2+): muscle/nerve function, metabolism (nuts, seeds, whole grains, dark leafy greens).
* Chloride (Cl-): digestion (stomach acid).
* Bicarbonate (HCO3-): acid-base regulation.
* Phosphorus (PO4³-): bone/teeth, ATP (meat, dairy).
* Sulfate (SO4²-): connective tissue formation (eggs, broccoli).

Trace minerals (selected)

* Iron: oxygen transport. Heme (animal) more bioavailable than non-heme (plant). Vitamin C enhances non-heme absorption.
* Zinc: enzyme function, immunity, wound healing (oysters, beef, nuts).
* Selenium: antioxidant, thyroid metabolism (Brazil nuts, seafood).
* Copper, manganese, iodine, chromium, molybdenum — various roles.

Boron

* Supports bone health, hormone balance, cognition, anti-inflammatory effects.
* Sources: nuts, fruits, vegetables, legumes.
* No official RDA; common safe intake 1–3 mg/day; >20 mg/day may be toxic.

Other nutrients

* Fluoride: trace element; present in tea, coffee; topical toothpaste use remineralizes enamel.
* Choline: essential for brain and liver health; found in eggs, liver, fish; supports acetylcholine and membrane phospholipids.
* Polyphenols: plant compounds (flavonoids, phenolic acids, stilbenes, lignans) with antioxidant/anti-inflammatory properties; not essential but beneficial.
* Antioxidants: vitamins C & E, carotenoids, flavonoids help neutralize free radicals and reduce oxidative stress.

### Antioxidants & Anti-Inflammatory Nutrients

Antioxidants

* Neutralize free radicals (unstable molecules missing electrons) to reduce oxidative stress.
* Foods: berries, citrus, nuts, seeds, dark leafy greens, dark chocolate, green tea, coffee.
* Examples: Vitamin C, Vitamin E, beta-carotene, flavonoids.

Astaxanthin + Full-spectrum Vitamin E (ProLife)

* Discussed supplement with astaxanthin (powerful antioxidant) + tocotrienols (vitamin E fraction).
* Suggested usage: 1–3 capsules/day; potential saturation phase for 1–2 months before maintenance.
* Claimed benefits: skin protection (internal sunscreen), hair growth, cardiovascular/brain benefits (as discussed in original content).

Anti-inflammatory nutrients

* Omega-3s, polyphenols (olive oil, berries, green tea, turmeric/curcumin), ginger, garlic.

### Calories & Energy Utilization

**Key points**

* A calorie is a unit of energy; calorie counting is a tool but oversimplifies nutrition.
* Nutrient balance and food matrix matter more than calories alone.
* Calorie labels refer to the food's potential energy, not necessarily what the body absorbs — cooking, processing, and resistant starch affect absorption (variation \~±10% or more).
* "Empty calories" = high energy but low micronutrient content.

**Factors affecting calorie utilization**

* Food preparation & processing (cooking increases digestibility and available energy).
* Resistant starch (e.g., cooled potatoes) reduces absorbable calories.
* Individual differences (microbiome, digestion speed).
* Portion estimation errors.

**Conclusion on calorie counting**

* Useful for short-term goals and learning baseline energy needs, but not a sustainable sole approach for long term. Combine with regular activity and stress management.

### Food Quality & Sourcing

* Food matrix and processing impact metabolic response: minimally processed whole foods are metabolized more favorably.
* Proper storage & adherence to expiration dates are important.
* Animal product quality (pasture-raised, natural feed) affects nutrient composition.
* Many processed meats contain additives — inspect ingredient lists.
* Eggs: hen welfare & diet influence yolk composition.
* Butter: grass-fed cows produce butter with different fatty acid profiles.

Hidden risks

* Natural foods may carry pollutants (pesticides, mycotoxins) — choose quality sourcing when possible.

### Practical Recommendations

* Avoid processed foods; choose whole foods (single/few ingredients).
* Balance macronutrients at meals (protein, fats, carbs).
* Diversify foods to cover micronutrient range.
* Time carbs around activity for sedentary people: consume higher-carb foods later in day or around workouts.
* Prepare grains/seeds/legumes properly (soaking, fermenting) to reduce antinutrients.
* Monitor blood markers (vitamin D, B12, iron, HbA1c) periodically.
* Avoid high-GI meals to reduce acne and post-meal tiredness.
* Move after meals to reduce glucose spikes.
* Avoid late-night eating; finish dinner 2–3 hours before bed when possible.
* Moderate fiber intake across day; avoid high fiber right before bed (can cause discomfort).
* Introduce fiber gradually to reduce flatulence.

Quick meal tips

* Eat vegetables first in a meal, then protein/fats, finish with starch/sugar — can reduce glucose spike by up to \~75%.
* Savory breakfasts preferred for non-exercisers.
* Pair fruit with nuts or yogurt to slow sugar absorption.

### Cooking & Food Preparation

{% step %}
### Improved digestibility

{% endstep %}

{% step %}
### Pathogen reduction

Heat kills bacteria, parasites, and viruses in raw meats, eggs, and some vegetables.
{% endstep %}

{% step %}
### Better flavor & texture

Maillard reactions and caramelization develop palatable flavors and textures.
{% endstep %}

{% step %}
### Reduced antinutrients

Cooking reduces lectins, phytic acid, and protease inhibitors in beans, grains, and legumes.
{% endstep %}

### Increased availability of certain nutrients

Lycopene (tomatoes) and beta-carotene (carrots) become more accessible after cooking.
{% endstep %}
{% endstepper %}

{% hint style="info" %}
Overcooking destroys heat-sensitive vitamins (C, some B vitamins). Use steaming/grilling to preserve nutrients and avoid high temperatures that create HCAs/PAHs.
{% endhint %}

Cooking prep: Soaking & rinsing

* Rice: rinse to remove surface starch (less sticky) and reduce arsenic (\~30–40% possible); soak to reduce phytic acid and GI.
* Quinoa/Buckwheat: rinse to remove saponins.
* Legumes: soak 8–12 h, discard soaking water, reduces phytates & lectins and gas.
* Nuts/seeds: short soak reduces enzyme inhibitors and phytic acid (dry/roast after).
* Potatoes: soak cut pieces to reduce surface starch and acrylamide formation when frying.

Proplachování rýže (Czech notes)

* Rinsing rice removes surface starch, reduces arsenic, improves texture.
* Soaking shortens cooking time, reduces phytates, improves digestibility, may lower GI.

High-heat cooking & oils

* Oil acts as thermal interface; avocado oil and ghee are heat-stable for high heat.
* Extra virgin olive oil (EVOO) contains polyphenols; moderate cooking (e.g., quick sauté) preserves much of its benefit if not overheated. EVOO is fine for short sautéing of ground beef at medium heat.

### Antinutrients — Overview & Mitigation

Common antinutrients

* Phytates: bind minerals (iron, zinc, magnesium); found in whole grains, legumes, nuts, seeds.
* Lectins: can interfere with digestion; concentrated in legumes, grains, nightshades.
* Oxalates: bind calcium; found in spinach, rhubarb, beets.
* Tannins: reduce iron and protein absorption; in tea, coffee, chocolate.
* Protease inhibitors: hinder digestive enzymes; in legumes & grains.

Reduction methods

* Soaking, cooking, sprouting, fermentation, and milling reduce antinutrient content and improve nutrient availability.

Practical note

* Most people don't need to avoid these foods entirely. Proper preparation and dietary diversity mitigate risks.

### Chewing, Satiety & Eating Behavior

* Chewing thoroughly (e.g., 40 vs 15 times/bite) can reduce calorie intake (\~15%).
* Mindful eating enhances digestion and satiety.
* Avoid combining dense meals with certain fruits (e.g., bananas) right after — can cause slower gastric emptying and fermentation/gassing for some individuals.
* Plant foods contain antinutrients — peeling some items (e.g., potatoes) can help in certain contexts.

Respectful eating

* How you eat (calm, seated, mindful) matters for digestion and metabolic responses.

***

### Meal Frequency & Nutrient Timing

General

* Spacing: evenly spaced servings containing \~20–30 g protein can support muscle protein synthesis.
* Results are often similar regardless of meal count; individual preference matters.
* Nutritional timing and composition influence metabolic responses (e.g., avoid high-carb evenings for sedentary people).

Nighttime & fasting recommendations

* Nighttime fasting: aim to finish dinner earlier (e.g., by 8 PM) and maintain \~12-hour overnight fast when feasible.
* Intermittent fasting can improve insulin sensitivity, autophagy, and digestion in many individuals.
* Avoid heavy, sugary, or high-GI meals in the evening; prefer lean proteins.

Hydration timing

* Majority of fluid intake earlier in the day; reduce fluids in evening to avoid sleep disruption.
* Electrolytes (sodium, potassium, magnesium) are essential for hydration and water transport into cells.
* Urine color as a hydration indicator: light yellow = good.

Pre- and post-workout

* Pre-workout (1–2 h): emphasize easily digestible carbs + small protein, minimal fat & fiber (banana + small nut butter; Greek yogurt + berries; oatmeal with protein).
* Post-workout: for most hobby athletes, immediate feeding is not critical; eat within 1–4 hours. Prioritize carbs + quality protein to replenish glycogen and repair muscle; avoid high fat immediately after intense exercise.

Fasted training

* May increase fat oxidation during the session; overall 24-hour fat loss depends on total energy balance.
* Individual preference and training goals determine utility.

Anabolic window & nighttime muscle maintenance

* Casein (30–40 g) before sleep provides slow amino acid release (6–8 h).
* Aim for \~2.5–3 g leucine pre-sleep for muscle protein synthesis.
* Glycine (3 g) + magnesium (300–400 mg) before bed may improve sleep and recovery.
* Avoid sugary high-GI foods and alcohol before bed.

Eat in the right order

* Veggies → protein & fats → starch/sugar reduces glucose spikes.

### Supplements (summary & guidance)

Principles

* Know each supplement’s purpose.
* Core daily essentials: omega-3, vitamin D3, magnesium (forms chosen by goal).
* Use saturation phase then maintenance when starting new supplements.
* Cycle adaptogens/performance aids when appropriate.
* Choose quality products.

Selected supplements (high-level notes)

* Creatine: 3–5 g/day standard; improves strength, cognition under stress; take with carbs or food to improve uptake.
* Caffeine: effective stimulant; 200–400 mg/day max for most; avoid habitual caffeine during peak morning cortisol to reduce tolerance; best between \~9:30–11:30 AM. Morning water before coffee recommended.
* Whey protein: fast absorbing; useful for post-workout; mixing into porridge/food increases satiety.
* Glutamine: gut support; dosages vary by purpose (5–15 g/day commonly used).
* Magnesium: forms vary (bisglycinate/threonate good for evening/sleep; malate for morning/energy; oxide less recommended).
* Omega-3: daily ongoing use recommended; EPA/DHA targets \~250–500 mg/day (context-dependent).
* Adaptogens: maca, ashwagandha, rhodiola, ginseng — used for stress, energy, libido; effects vary by herb.
* Curcumin, black pepper extract (piperine — increases absorption), green tea extract, guarana — metabolic & antioxidant effects.
* Astaxanthin + tocotrienols: antioxidant combo discussed (dosing 1–3 capsules/day in source).

Peptides / SARMs / Steroids

* Peptides: broad class; therapeutic peptides exist (e.g., insulin) and topical peptides in cosmetics. Synthetic growth/release peptides carry risks and are often unregulated for non-therapeutic use.
* SARMs & anabolic steroids: significant side effects; long-term risks; legal and safety concerns.
* GLP-1 agonists (e.g., semaglutide): weight loss includes lean mass loss (\~up to 40% of weight lost can be lean mass) — important to consider body composition.

Safety

* Prefer evidence-based, high-quality products.
* Medical supervision for hormone/peptide/steroid interventions.
* Supplements are adjuncts; whole-food diet and lifestyle are primary.

### Glycine — Key notes

* Typical studied regimens: up to 10–15 g/day (speaker’s regimen \~15 g/day).
* Sleep: 3 g before bed.
* Safe in wide ranges; benefits include collagen support, sleep, metabolic effects, reduction of visceral fat in animal models, and antioxidant support.

### Intermittent Fasting & Longevity Notes

Benefits

* Improves insulin sensitivity, promotes autophagy, reduces inflammation, can enhance cognition and focus.
* Timing meals earlier in the day (time-restricted feeding / early eating) may align with circadian rhythms and improve sleep and heart rate variability.
* Fasted training increases metabolic flexibility but does not guarantee greater long-term fat loss compared to matched calories.

Practical

* Use coffee and electrolytes to manage hunger during fasting windows if desired.
* Monitor appetite as an indicator of effectiveness (reduced appetite may indicate benefit).

***

### Keto / Low-carb insights (selected)

* Ketones (BHB, AcAc, acetone) are alternative fuels produced when glucose is low.
* Ketogenic states can improve clarity, reduce insulin, decrease inflammation, and support mitochondrial function.
* Ketone supplements exist but do not replace metabolic benefits of low insulin via diet/fasting.
* Context matters: athletes, age, and metabolic health influence appropriateness of keto.

***

### Harmful / Disrupting Ingredients

* Many food additives (E-numbers) and industrial sweeteners can be detrimental in excess.
* Fructose-glucose syrup (high-fructose corn syrup) and high added sugars contribute to obesity, insulin resistance, fatty liver.
* Non-caloric sweeteners may still provoke physiological insulin responses or cephalic phase responses; effects vary by sweetener (stevia may not spike insulin).

***

### Superfoods & Notable Foods

Selected nutrient-dense foods mentioned:

* Eggs, berries, spinach, flax/chia seeds, nuts (macadamia, walnut, hazelnut, cashew), avocado, green tea, Greek yogurt, cacao (90% dark), cinnamon, salmon (and other fatty fish), pepper (piperine), dates, extra virgin olive oil (EVOO).

Olive oil benefits (EVOO)

* Reduces post-meal oxidative stress, supports weight management, lowers blood pressure, improves glycemic control, beneficial lipid effects, supports brain health, and mood — largely due to monounsaturated fats and polyphenols.

***

### Final Practical Tips & Reminders

* Prioritize whole foods and minimize ultra-processed foods.
* Combine carbs with protein/fat/fiber to blunt glucose spikes.
* Move after eating (e.g., 10 minutes of light activity).
* Be mindful of portion estimates if calorie tracking; calorie counting has limited long-term sustainability.
* Maintain food variety and rotate foods for micronutrient coverage.
* Tailor timing, macronutrient distribution, and supplements to individual goals, activity levels, and health status.
* When in doubt or considering potent interventions (peptides, hormones, GLP‑1, steroids), consult a medical professional.
